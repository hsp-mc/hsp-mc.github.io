PROJECT OVERVIEW

SPHERE THERMAL INSULATION EXPERIMENT

This experiment evaluates the transient cooling behaviour of a water-filled test capsule with different insulation configurations. Measured temperature histories are compared with a first-order Newton cooling model.

The project provides a simplified study of thermal resistance, experimental uncertainty and model validation. It is inspired by multilayer thermal-control systems, but the ice-water bath is a terrestrial conductive and convective environment rather than a simulation of vacuum.

The warm water represents the protected thermal mass, while the ice-water bath provides a repeatable low-temperature boundary condition.

Before testing, calculate a reference cooling curve. During the experiment, record temperatures at fixed intervals and quantify model agreement using residuals and mean absolute error (MAE).

Learning outcomes: Apply lumped-parameter heat-transfer modelling, construct a controlled test article, acquire temperature data and evaluate model limitations.

HEAT-TRANSFER MECHANISMS IN THE TEST ASSEMBLY

COTTON SHEETS — REDUCING CONDUCTION

Mechanism: Cotton contains many small air pockets. Because air is a poor conductor of heat, cotton slows the transfer of thermal energy from the warm container to the colder surroundings.

BUBBLE WRAP — REDUCING CONVECTION

Mechanism: Bubble wrap traps air inside sealed pockets. Because the air cannot move freely, convection currents are reduced and heat is retained more effectively.

MYLAR FOIL — REDUCING RADIATION

Mechanism: Reflective film has low emissivity and can reduce radiative heat transfer when separated by an air gap. Flight multilayer insulation uses specialised reflective layers and spacers under vacuum; the classroom assembly is not equivalent to flight hardware.

POTENTIAL PHYSICAL & EXPERIMENTAL CAUSES FOR GRAPH DISCREPANCY
1. Material Model / Constant ($k$) Mismatch

Symptom: Measured curve slope decays significantly faster or slower than predicted.
Cause: Selected reference model (Bare, Bubble Wrap, Mylar, Foam, Aerogel) or custom $k$ value does not reflect the actual insulator being tested.

2. Room Air Currents & Drafts

Symptom: Temperatures systematically lower than model prediction.
Cause: Forced air currents or ventilation increase convective heat transfer coefficient ($h$). Check if ambient room temperature ($T_{\text{env}}$) fluctuated during trial.

3. Unsealed Lid & Evaporative Cooling

Symptom: Rapid early drop in temperature exceeding linear conduction predictions.
Cause: Unsealed stopper, loose lid, or open thermocouple port allows water vapor escape. Evaporation removes thermal energy far faster than pure surface conduction.

4. Thermometer Placement & Fluid Stratification

Symptom: Reading scatter, spikes, or unexpected slope fluctuations.
Cause: Sensor touching vessel glass wall instead of fluid core depth, or un-stirred water creating thermal stratification (hot water floating over cooler fluid).

5. Initial Glass Wall Thermal Mass (0–2 min)

Symptom: Steeper temperature drop specifically during the first 2 minutes.
Cause: Cold flask absorbing energy before reaching thermal equilibrium. Newton's Law assumes a lumped capacitance system with uniform container temperature.

BOARDWORK KEYFRAMES & EQUATIONS MATH_THEORY // DIRECTIVE

Instructor Guidance

Use these explanations to anchor class theories on the chalkboard:

1. SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITY

Calculates total energy loss from internal core volume to surroundings:

$$Q = m \cdot c \cdot \Delta T$$

Where: $m$ = $0.1\text{ kg}$ ($100\text{ mL}$ water), $c$ = $4184\text{ J/kg}\cdot^\circ\text{C}$ (Specific heat capacity of water), $\Delta T$ = Temperature change. This equation calculates the amount of thermal energy gained or lost by water when its temperature changes.

2. NEWTON'S LAW OF COOLING

Models the rate of heat loss proportional to temperature differences:

$$T(t) = T_{\text{env}} + (T_0 - T_{\text{env}}) \cdot e^{-kt}$$

Where: $T_0$ = $80^\circ\text{C}$, $T_{\text{env}}$ = $0^\circ\text{C}$ (ice bath), $k$ = insulation cooling constant, $t$ = time. For this project, students do not need to derive the equation. The model is used to explore how insulation affects cooling behaviour and how mathematical predictions compare with experimental data.

INTERACTIVE PRACTICE: HOW TO CALCULATE YOUR DECAY CONSTANT (k)

STEP-BY-STEP MATH TOOL

The decay constant $k$ measures how fast heat leaks out of your capsule. You can calculate your capsule's exact $k$-value from your lab thermometer readings using this formula derived from Newton's Law of Cooling:

$$k = -\frac{\ln\left(\frac{T(t) - T_{\text{env}}}{T_0 - T_{\text{env}}}\right)}{t}$$
CALCULATED RESULTS & STEP-BY-STEP BREAKDOWN:
$$\text{Temp Ratio} = \frac{43.9 - 0.0}{80.0 - 0.0} = 0.5488$$
$$\ln(0.5488) = -0.6003$$
$$k = -\frac{-0.6003}{15} = 0.040\text{ min}^{-1}$$
CLOSEST ASSUMED REFERENCE: BUBBLE-WRAP TEST ASSEMBLY ($k \approx 0.040\text{ min}^{-1}$)

PRACTICE PROBLEMS FOR CLASSROOM REINFORCEMENT

PRACTICE PROBLEM #01
An astronaut capsule is filled with $T_0 = 80^\circ\text{C}$ hot water and placed into a $T_{\text{env}} = 0^\circ\text{C}$ ice bath. After $t = 15\text{ min}$, its thermometer reads $T(15) = 63.8^\circ\text{C}$. What is the capsule's insulation decay constant $k$?
💡 Hint: Extract values: $T_0 = 80^\circ\text{C}$, $T_{\text{env}} = 0^\circ\text{C}$, $T(15) = 63.8^\circ\text{C}$, $t = 15\text{ min}$. Plug into:
$$k = -\frac{\ln\left(\frac{63.8 - 0}{80 - 0}\right)}{15}$$
✓ Solution:
$$\text{Temp Ratio} = \frac{63.8}{80} = 0.7975 \implies \ln(0.7975) = -0.2263$$
$$k = -\frac{-0.2263}{15} = 0.015\text{ min}^{-1}$$
This matches the assumed cooling constant for the multilayer test configuration.
PRACTICE PROBLEM #02
If a Mylar-wrapped capsule has $k = 0.121\text{ min}^{-1}$ and starts at $T_0 = 80^\circ\text{C}$ in a $T_{\text{env}} = 0^\circ\text{C}$ ice bath, what will its temperature be after $t = 20\text{ minutes}$?
💡 Hint: Use Newton's cooling formula:
$$T(20) = 0 + (80 - 0) \cdot e^{-0.121 \times 20}$$
✓ Solution:
$$e^{-0.121 \times 20} = e^{-2.42} = 0.0889$$
$$T(20) = 80 \times 0.0889 = 7.1^\circ\text{C}$$
