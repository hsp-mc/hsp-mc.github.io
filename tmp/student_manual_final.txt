1. ENGINEERING CONTEXT & OBJECTIVES
Spacecraft thermal control must account for radiative exchange, internal heat generation and highly variable
external heat fluxes. In vacuum there is no external convection; thermal design therefore differs fundamentally
from this water-bath experiment.
Aerospace multilayer insulation uses low-emissivity reflective layers separated by low-conductance spacers.
The cotton, bubble wrap and reflective film used here form an educational multilayer assembly, not flightqualified MLI.
Test the assigned insulation configuration under a common boundary condition. Compare observations with
the analytical reference curve and report residuals, MAE, uncertainty sources and model limitations.

4. THE PHYSICS: NEWTON'S LAW OF COOLING
Heat always flows from warmer objects to cooler surroundings. Over 300 years ago, Sir Isaac Newton
discovered that the rate at which an object cools is proportional to the temperature difference between the
object and its environment:

NEWTON'S LAW OF COOLING (EXPONENTIAL DECAY MODEL)

T (t) = Tenv + (T0 − Tenv ) ⋅ e−k⋅t
​

​

​

Where:
T (t) = Predicted core temperature (∘ C) at minute t
Tenv = Ice bath temperature (0.0∘ C)
T0 = Starting core temperature (80.0∘ C)
k = Cooling rate constant (min−1 )
​

​

Interpretation of k: The fitted cooling constant k has units of min−1 . Under otherwise identical conditions, a
smaller value corresponds to a slower approach to the bath temperature.

STAGE 4 • BOUNDARY SINK IMMERSION

read the observed temperature (Tobs ) and immediately enter it into your

interface.

6. SOLUTION KEY
Use this section after completing the Student Task Sheet. The model answers correspond to Inquiries 1-10 and
explain the physical reasoning expected in a complete response.

Question 1: Radiant Barriers vs Direct Conduction
Answer: Reflective film reduces radiative heat exchange, but it is very thin and provides little resistance to
conduction when it touches the capsule and ice water directly. A low-conductivity spacer such as cotton or
trapped air is needed to interrupt the conductive heat-flow path.

Question 2: Understanding the Decay Constant k
Answer: A smaller fitted value of k means better insulation under otherwise identical conditions. In T (t) = Tenv +
(T0 − Tenv )e−kt , a smaller k makes the exponential term decrease more slowly, so the capsule approaches the
bath temperature more gradually.
​

​

​

Question 3: Why Multilayer Insulation Works
Answer: The layers act through complementary mechanisms. Cotton traps air and reduces conduction, bubble
wrap limits fluid motion and convection, and Mylar reduces radiative exchange. The interfaces between layers also
add contact resistance, producing the smallest reference cooling constant (k ≈ 0.015 min−1 ).

Question 4: Real World Experimental Factors
Answer: Any two plausible factors include the probe touching the colder capsule wall, leakage through an
incomplete lid seal, the ice bath warming above its assumed temperature, inconsistent immersion depth, delayed
timing, or internal thermal stratification when the capsule water is not mixed.

Question 5: Ice Bath Temperature Drift
Answer: Measure the actual bath temperature and change Tenv in Mission Control from 0.0∘ C to the measured
value, such as 4.5∘ C. Recalculate the theoretical curve using that updated boundary temperature.
​

Question 6: Effect of Capsule Water Volume
Answer: Increasing the water volume from 100 mL to 200 mL approximately doubles the thermal mass because
Q = mcp ΔT . With more stored energy and roughly the same external area, the capsule cools more slowly and
the fitted effective value of k decreases.
​

Question 7: Spacesuit Design Trade Offs
Answer: Unlimited layers would increase mass, bulk and stiffness, restricting astronaut joint movement. A suit
must also reject metabolic heat; excessive insulation without controlled heat removal could cause dangerous heat
buildup.

Question 8: Specific Heat Capacity and Fluid Response
Answer: A liquid with lower specific heat capacity stores less thermal energy for the same mass and temperature
rise. Under the same heat-transfer conditions, its temperature falls faster than the temperature of water.

Question 9: Asymptotic Boundary Limit
Answer: As t → ∞, e−kt → 0. Therefore T (t) approaches Tenv , which is the bath temperature. For the nominal
reference case, the asymptotic temperature is 0.0∘ C.
​

Question 10: Telemetry Anomaly Diagnostics and MAE
Answer: A temperature drop much faster than predicted most strongly suggests cold-water leakage into the
capsule or extra fluid motion that increases convection. Reduce MAE by sealing the lid, centring the probe, keeping
immersion depth and bath temperature consistent, and recording at exact 60-second intervals.
