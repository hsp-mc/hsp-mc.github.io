Executive Summary
This comprehensive engineering report details the end-to-end development, theoretical
modeling, and empirical validation of the "Spacesuit Thermal Shielding Kit," an educa-
tional science module designed to teach the principles of Multi-Layer Insulation (MLI)
and spacesuit thermodynamics to secondary school students. Developed as a core module
for the European SPHERE (Space Schools Exploration and Research Mission) Project,
our team at the Technical University of Munich (TUM) engineered a robust, high-fidelity,
low-cost (approximately €35) analog experiment.

Maximizing scalability for underfunded educational institutions across Europe, we uti-
lized a completely off-the-shelf hardware approach. This methodology features industrial
PG7 cable gland seals to simulate cryogenic astronaut survival safely without relying
on specialized or expensive 3D printing infrastructure. The physical hardware is paired
with a custom web-based "Mission Control Logbook" application, providing an interac-
tive data-logging and graphing experience that deepens pedagogical engagement through
active scientific observation. This report outlines the theoretical background governing
orbital heat transfer, mechanical design constraints for watertight testing, software archi-
tecture for the telemetry interface, and the extensive laboratory validation that proves
the educational efficacy and safety of the final prototype.


Introduction and Objectives
The fascination with human spaceflight serves as a unique and highly effective gateway
to STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) education. Traditional
physics curricula often struggle to contextualize abstract thermodynamic principles in a
way that resonates with secondary school students. The primary objective of this project
was to create a science kit that translates complex aerospace engineering principles into
a didacticly sound, hands-on classroom experiment.


Project Constraints and Educational Goals
The development of this kit was guided by several strict constraints established during
the project kickoff:

  • Feasibility Constraint: The kit must align with standard school curricula, utilize
    highly accessible hardware that can be purchased globally, and strictly not exceed
    a €50.00 budget limit per kit.

  • Engagement Constraint: The experiment must utilize high-tech narratives,
    specifically mirroring the extreme thermal challenges faced by astronauts wearing

The SPHERE Project Context
The SPHERE project is a joint initiative between TUM and the Austrian Space Forum.
Its core mission is to create an accessible "toolkit for teachers" that simplifies complex
space missions into standardized, highly engaging lesson plans.

Our Thermal Shielding Kit serves as a core physical module within this broader pedagog-
ical ecosystem. By tying our specific thermodynamics experiment to the SPHERE analog
mission narrative, students are not merely learning about heat transfer in a vacuum; they
are actively role-playing as mission control engineers responsible for the survival of their
simulated astronauts. This context transforms a standard physics lab into an immersive,
high-stakes engineering challenge. Students are evaluated not just on reaching a "correct"
answer, but on their iterative engineering process, their ability to analyze telemetry data,
and their justification for material selection in their thermal shield designs.


Theoretical Background: Thermodynamics in Space
To accurately simulate the space environment within a classroom setting, it is necessary
to abstract the thermal conditions of Low Earth Orbit (LEO). In the vacuum of space,
the absence of a fluid medium means that heat transfer via conduction and convection is
negligible; radiation dominates thermal dynamics. However, in our school-based analog,
we simulate the extreme cold of a shadowed orbital environment (which can drop to
−150◦ C in LEO) using an ice-water bath (0◦ C). Therefore, the analog kit must teach
students how to mitigate all three forms of heat transfer simultaneously.


Mathematical Modeling of Heat Loss
The total thermal energy contained within the simulated astronaut (the warm water
capsule) is calculated using the specific heat capacity formula:

                                      Qtotal = mc∆T                                     (1)

Where m is the mass of the water (100g), c is the specific heat capacity of water
(4.184 J/g◦ C), and ∆T is the temperature change over the 15-minute operational window.

Hardware Design and Manufacturing
Initial prototyping focused on utilizing TUM’s internal 3D printing resources. However,
to ensure the kit could be deployed to hundreds of underfunded schools without relying
on specialized infrastructure, we engineered a massively scalable solution utilizing highly
reliable, off-the-shelf industrial components.


Material Science of the Testing Capsules
The primary testing vessel must withstand repeated exposure to 80◦ C water without
degrading, warping, or leaching chemicals. We selected 100ml Polyethylene Terephthalate
(PET) jars. PET was chosen over standard Polystyrene or low-density polyethylene due
to its higher glass transition temperature (Tg ≈ 70 − 80◦ C) and superior dimensional
stability under thermal stress. This ensures the screw threads remain perfectly aligned
even when heated.


The Cable Gland Seal Strategy
The core mechanical engineering challenge was passing a digital sensor wire into the
warm water capsule without allowing the internal water to leak into the external ice
bath, nor allowing the electronic housing to short circuit. We utilized a standard PG7
Cable Gland (IP68 rated). By drilling a precise 12.5mm port into the PET lid, we
threaded the gland through. The PG7 gland features a trapezoidal thread and a nitrile
rubber (NBR) compression gasket. When the outer dome nut is torqued down, the rubber
gasket compresses radially around the thermometer wire. This achieves a pressure-rated,
100% waterproof seal capable of withstanding the internal pressure fluctuations caused
by the thermal expansion and contraction of the heated water.


Sensor Selection
We utilize standard NTC (Negative Temperature Coefficient) thermistor probes attached
to external LCD displays. These provide a measurement range of −50◦ C to 110◦ C with
a margin of error of ±1◦ C, which is more than sufficient for high school laboratory
environments.

Software Integration: The Web-Based Logbook
To fulfill the project’s requirement for extensive documentation and modern interactivity,
we developed a lightweight, browser-based web application hosted securely via GitHub
Pages.


Architecture Selection
We explicitly rejected building a native iOS/Android mobile application or an auto-
mated Bluetooth logging system. In educational environments, requiring teachers to
install custom applications on student devices, or relying on strict school IT networks to
allow Bluetooth pairing, introduces unacceptable points of failure. By utilizing standard
HTML5, CSS3, and Vanilla JavaScript, the "Mission Control Logbook" is a true Single
Page Application (SPA) that runs entirely client-side. It is universally accessible on any
device with a web browser, requiring zero installation.


Pedagogical Telemetry Approach
Instead of automated data logging, our software mandates manual telemetry. Students
must physically read the LCD screen of their thermometer and manually input the tem-
perature data into the web app every 60 seconds. While less technologically advanced,
this design choice enforces active scientific observation. It keeps students cognitively en-
gaged throughout the entire 15-minute runtime of the experiment, rather than passively
watching a screen do the work for them.


Data Visualization Engine
Utilizing the open-source Chart.js JavaScript library, the web app dynamically plots the
cooling curve (Temperature vs. Time) in real-time. As students input their readings,
the DOM updates instantly, animating the curve. This immediate visual feedback allows
students to instantly see the mathematical difference between the steep decay curve of
an uninsulated control capsule and the flattened curve of their custom MLI design.

Safety and Risk Mitigation
Safety is paramount when delivering physical kits to unsupervised high school environ-
ments. We conducted a comprehensive risk matrix analysis during the development phase
to identify and mitigate potential hazards.


Hazard Identification and Protocols
  • Electrical Hazards (Low Severity, Low Likelihood): Mitigated by using ex-
    clusively low-voltage (1.5V button cell) battery-operated digital thermometers. The
    IP68 rated PG7 cable gland ensures absolute physical separation of the internal wa-
    ter and the external wire contacts. Even in the event of a catastrophic seal failure,
    1.5V is imperceptible and entirely safe.

  • Thermal Hazards (Medium Severity, Medium Likelihood): Boiling wa-
    ter (100◦ C) presents a severe scalding risk. Therefore, the experimental protocol
    strictly caps the starting water temperature at 80◦ C. This provides enough thermal
    energy to produce a highly measurable, steep decay curve for the experiment, while
    significantly reducing the risk of third-degree burns in the event of a spill.

  • Mechanical Hazards (Low Severity, Low Likelihood): By eliminating 3D
    printing, we removed the risk of sharp plastic layer-lines and delamination. The
    off-the-shelf PET jars are shatter-proof if dropped on a hard classroom floor, unlike
    traditional glass laboratory beakers.


Budget and Financial Feasibility
The total cost per unit was rigorously minimized to ensure the project is viable for broad
distribution to public schools, which often operate under severe budgetary constraints.

By sourcing standardized PET jars (€1.50) and PG7 Cable Glands (€5.00 for a multi-
pack) alongside highly reusable analog materials (Mylar, felt, bubble wrap), the total
cost per kit averages €35.20. This comfortably beats the €50.00 feasibility threshold
mandated by the course guidelines. Furthermore, all components are highly durable; the
recurring cost for a school to run this experiment in subsequent years is practically zero.

Testing and Validation Results
Extensive validation tests were performed in the TUM laboratories to ensure the kit
functioned predictably, safely, and accurately within a standard 45-minute school lesson
block.


Seal Integrity Validation
The PG7 modified capsules were subjected to extreme thermal shock testing. Capsules
were filled with 80◦ C water, sealed, and immediately submerged fully in a 0◦ C ice bath.
They were left submerged for 24 hours to test for weeping or capillary action along the
sensor wire. Zero ingress of ice water or egress of internal fluid was detected across four
separate test units, proving the mechanical reliability of the nitrile rubber compression
gasket.


Thermal Modeling Accuracy
We established a theoretical decay curve using Newton’s Law of Cooling, factoring in the
combined thermal resistance of the MLI. During physical testing, the 3-layer MLI setup
(Cotton base, Bubble Wrap mid-layer, Mylar outer shell) maintained the internal water
temperature remarkably well. The empirical data tracked within a 4.8% margin of error
compared to the theoretical mathematical decay curve modeled by our team.


Experiment Duration and Contrast
For the educational impact to be significant, the visual difference between a "good" space-
suit and a "bad" spacesuit must be obvious within a short timeframe. During our 15-
minute validation tests, the uninsulated control capsule dropped by 45◦ C. In stark con-
trast, the fully insulated MLI capsule dropped by only 12◦ C. This massive delta (∆33◦ C)
ensures that students running the experiment will see exciting, measurable results quickly,
validating their engineering design choices in real-time.


Conclusion
The Spacesuit Thermal Shielding Kit successfully bridges the gap between advanced
aerospace engineering and secondary education. By pivoting from custom 3D printed
hardware to a highly scalable, off-the-shelf sealing methodology, and pairing it with a ro-
bust interactive web logbook, our team has delivered a comprehensive educational pack-
age. It perfectly aligns with the SPHERE mission’s goal of increasing STEM fascination
through the lens of human spaceflight.

Mathematical Modeling and Thermodynamics
My primary contribution to the Spacesuit Thermal Shielding Kit was establishing the
theoretical mathematical framework and conducting the thermodynamic modeling that
dictated our hardware choices. Before any physical components were purchased or man-
ufactured, it was necessary to prove that the experiment would actually work within the
constraints of a high school classroom—specifically, that a measurable, dramatic temper-
ature drop would occur within a strict 15-minute educational window.

To achieve this, I utilized Newton’s Law of Cooling as our baseline model for the unin-
sulated control capsule. The rate of heat loss is proportional to the difference in temper-
atures between the body and its surroundings:

                              T (t) = Tenv + (T0 − Tenv )e−kt                          (2)

Where T0 is the initial temperature of the warm water (80◦ C), Tenv is the ice bath
(0◦ C), and k is the cooling constant. I derived the theoretical k value by calculating
the total surface area of our 100ml cylindrical capsule. The surface area is given by
A = 2πrh + 2πr2 . For our specific PET jars (r = 2.25cm, h = 6.5cm), the surface
area is approximately 123.7cm2 . By cross-referencing this area with the known thermal
conductivity of standard PET plastic (0.15 W/m · K), I was able to model exactly how
fast the baseline capsule would bleed thermal energy into the ice bath.

Simulation Data Analysis and Material Optimization
Following the establishment of the uninsulated baseline, I modeled the expected thermal
resistance (R-value) of our proposed multi-layer insulation (MLI). This was a complex
systems-engineering challenge, as it required combining three different modes of heat
transfer resistance into a single effective mathematical model. I had to calculate the
conductive resistance of the cotton layer, the convective resistance of the bubble wrap
(treating the trapped air bubbles as a static, non-circulating fluid), and the radiative
resistance of the aluminized Mylar.

The total thermal resistance is the sum of these layers in series:

                            Rtotal = Rcotton + Rbubble + Rmylar                         (3)


By simulating these variables using mathematical modeling software, I was able to opti-
mize the required volume of water. The specific heat capacity of water means it acts as
a thermal battery. If we used too much water (e.g., 500ml), the thermal mass would be
too high. The temperature wouldn’t drop fast enough for students to notice a difference
within a 15-minute class period, leading to a boring experiment. Conversely, if we used
too little water (e.g., 20ml), it would cool too rapidly, creating a curve so steep that
manual data logging every 60 seconds would be impossible.

My calculations proved that exactly 100ml of water provided the perfect thermal decay
curve for this specific educational lab. I subsequently led the physical validation tests in
the TUM laboratory. I calibrated the thermistors, ran the physical experiments, and plot-
ted the empirical data against my MATLAB models. The real-world prototype matched
my mathematical models with a margin of error of exactly 4.8%. This tight alignment of
theoretical modeling with physical prototyping was a major success for our engineering
team and validated the entire premise of the kit.

Hardware Engineering and Sealing Integration
My core responsibility on this project was the mechanical design, physical integration, and
manufacturing strategy of the testing capsule. Initially, based on the kickoff presentation
guidelines, I drafted intricate CAD (Computer-Aided Design) models for a custom 3D-
printed capsule utilizing the TUM ASG resources in Ottobrunn. I designed a dual-
threaded lid mechanism with an internal dry-well for the sensor.

However, after conducting a rigorous tolerance analysis on standard FDM (Fused Depo-
sition Modeling) and SLA resin printing methods, I identified a critical, systemic failure
point. The microscopic layer lines inherent in 3D prints are highly prone to weeping.
When exposed to the severe thermal shock of 80◦ C water rapidly cooling in a 0◦ C environ-
ment, the plastic undergoes rapid thermal contraction. This micro-warping compromises
the layer adhesion, allowing water to slowly breach the hull.

To solve this and ensure the kit was massively scalable for the SPHERE project, I made
the executive engineering decision to pivot our manufacturing strategy entirely to off-the-
shelf components. I researched industrial sealing methods used in marine biology and
aerospace applications, which led me to implement the PG7 Cable Gland strategy. This
pivot completely transformed the viability of the project. Instead of requiring a school
to own, calibrate, and operate a 3D printer for 4 hours to produce a single capsule, they
simply needed to purchase cheap, highly durable, mass-produced industrial parts.

Tolerance Testing and Mechanical Manufacturing
Integrating the industrial cable gland required precise mechanical execution and tolerance
testing. I sourced standard 100ml PET (Polyethylene Terephthalate) plastic jars. I chose
PET specifically for its glass transition temperature properties; it safely exceeds our 80◦ C
testing limit without warping, off-gassing, or losing structural integrity, which is vital for
classroom safety.

The mechanical challenge was breaching the lid without destroying its structural rigidity.
Through iterative testing, I determined that a standard 12.5mm drill bit was the exact
tolerance required to thread the PG7 gland through the PET lid without cracking the
surrounding plastic flange.

Furthermore, I conducted extensive stress tests on the digital thermometer probes. The
wires connected to cheap LCD thermometers are notoriously fragile, utilizing very thin
gauge copper wire. By utilizing the specific nitrile rubber (NBR) compression gasket
inside the PG7 gland, I was able to calculate the exact torque required on the outer dome
nut. I secured the wire tightly enough to create a 100% waterproof, IP68-rated seal, but
gently enough to avoid severing the internal copper connections. This mechanical pivot
not only solved our leaking issue definitively but also reduced the total manufacturing
time per kit to just 5 minutes (the time it takes to drill the hole and assemble the gland).
This makes the kit infinitely better suited for mass distribution to schools across Europe
as part of the broader SPHERE initiative.

Web-Based Logbook Architecture
I was responsible for designing, architecting, and developing the software component of
our kit, fulfilling the course’s requirement for interactive digital documentation. Rec-
ognizing that asking high school teachers to install custom mobile apps (.apk files) or
navigate Bluetooth drivers on strict school IT networks is a massive barrier to entry, I
architected a highly accessible, lightweight, browser-based web platform.

The "Mission Control Logbook" was built completely from scratch using standard web
technologies: HTML5, CSS3, and Vanilla JavaScript. By explicitly avoiding heavy back-
end frameworks (like React, Angular, or Vue) and entirely omitting server-side databases,
I ensured the site is incredibly lightweight. It loads instantly and runs smoothly even
on ten-year-old school Chromebooks or cheap tablets. I deployed and hosted the plat-
form utilizing GitHub Pages. This ensures the platform remains free, open-source, and
accessible indefinitely without requiring ongoing server maintenance costs or domain reg-
istration fees from the university. This architecture aligns perfectly with the SPHERE
project’s ultimate goal of creating universally accessible toolkits for global educators.

UI/UX Design and Telemetry Implementation
A major focus of my software development work was on the User Interface (UI) and User
Experience (UX). The platform needed to visually replicate a high-tech aerospace dash-
board to engage the students, while remaining highly intuitive for educational purposes.
I implemented a dark-mode, space-themed aesthetic utilizing high-contrast neon accents

ware and the software in tandem, solidifying their fundamental understanding of data
collection, graphical representation, and telemetry analysis in STEM fields.

Pedagogy and Educational Alignment
My primary role on the team focused on the didactic framing, curriculum alignment, and
financial feasibility of the project. An engineering kit, no matter how technically brilliant,
is only as effective as the lesson plan that accompanies it. I structured the educational
documentation around the principles of Constructivist learning theory (specifically draw-
ing on Piaget’s models of cognitive development). This ensures students aren’t just
following a recipe blindly, but are actively forming hypotheses, testing variables, and
drawing conclusions based on their own physical observations.

I authored the "Mission Briefing" narrative and the "Inquiry Prompts" hosted on Dilan’s
web app. Instead of giving a dry instruction to "test insulation materials," the narrative
I designed frames the students as mission control engineers actively trying to save an
astronaut’s life from the freezing vacuum of space. I formulated specific prompts such as
"What happens to the decay curve if we remove the Mylar layer?" and "Why does the

of Newton’s Law of Cooling). Furthermore, I authored the comprehensive Teacher’s
Guide, detailing the setup instructions, learning objectives, and the explicit supervision
warnings required by strict European school safety standards.
